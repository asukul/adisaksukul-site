Workshop brief

Universities ranked above Iowa State have not agreed on one AI rule. They have agreed on a process. The institution supplies vocabulary and data rules, the instructor writes a course statement, and the real decisions happen assignment by assignment. In 2025–26 science and quantitative syllabi at MIT, Wharton, UW, UF and UW–Madison, the common pattern is: AI allowed for practice and learning, banned on proctored quizzes and exams, use disclosed in a line or two, and the student owns every error. Wherever an institution has set a default for a syllabus that says nothing, that default is "not allowed" (Maryland, Rutgers, Michigan State). The best student evidence shows that silence otherwise reads as permission. AI detectors have been switched off or disavowed almost everywhere.

The frontier is grounded course tutors. The two best-documented ones, Harvard's CS50 Duck and Georgia Tech's Jill Watson, both found that prompts alone cannot hold the guardrail, which is exactly what an instructor-run audit of a course tutor checks. No peer agriculture college publishes its own AI-in-teaching policy. No peer publishes an instructor workflow for "answer only from my materials." Instructors who ground a tutor in their own materials and audit it are ahead of the peers, not catching up.

Three syllabus sentences, assembled from peer language

May be used for. You may use ISU-licensed AI tools (logged-in Copilot, Gemini or NotebookLM) or this course's study guide to review concepts, quiz yourself and get feedback on drafts, after you have first attempted the work yourself.

May not be used for, and why. You may not use AI on quizzes and exams, or to write the interpretation you submit in lab reports, because those assignments are designed to assess your own understanding and independent thinking.

Not authoritative. AI output is not an authoritative source. It can fabricate credible-looking data, references, quotations and calculations, so check anything you use against course materials. You are responsible for every error you submit.

Where each sentence comes from: - Sentence 1 draws on UW CSE 373's allow-list, Missouri BIOSC 3750's list of roles (editor, translator, idea generator, data visualization tool, tutor) and Duke STA 199's "first attempt to solve the problem yourself." - Sentence 2 draws on MIT 14.05, UF ANS 3006, and UW Econ 200's rationale (UW Econ 200). - Sentence 3 draws on Missouri microbiology and Ethan Mollick, both undated entries in Eaton's repository (Missouri's rank relative to ISU was not verified), plus UChicago Harris.

Optional fourth line: "If an assignment does not say, assume AI is not allowed. Where it is allowed, add one line: tool, what for, and how you checked it."

What Iowa State already has. ISU does publish AI syllabus guidance, with three approaches, not four. CELT's "Gen AI Syllabus Considerations," linked from its AI in Teaching page, offers "Permitted and encouraged use," "Limited use" and "Prohibited use." Peers hand instructors a vocabulary, not a rule

Every peer leaves the permission decision to instructors. They differ in whether a syllabus statement is mandatory and in how much support the institution publishes. A few require a statement: - UT Austin: "Every syllabus must include a course AI policy." Instructors declare AI permitted, prohibited or partially permitted, and must give written disclosure instructions whenever AI is allowed (UT Austin Provost). - Nebraska–Lincoln (rank unverified): the Faculty Senate syllabus policy of November 4, 2025 requires a statement to "Describe whether AI is allowed for use in your course" (UNL Faculty Senate). - Duke: its 2026/2027 syllabus template now carries AI policy guidance from its Office of Undergraduate Education (Duke CTL).

ISU joined this small group in Fall 2026. Every undergraduate course must now carry an ISU Canvas template that includes "Instructor policies for attendance, late work, extra credit and use of AI." The provost calls completion "a component with the teaching portion of the faculty Position Responsibility Statement," and says non-compliance "will be reflected in annual reviews" (Inside Iowa State; ISU Provost).

Everyone else "strongly encourages" a statement. That includes Berkeley, Michigan, UW–Madison, Minnesota, Penn State, Maryland, Purdue, Harvard, Northwestern and Cornell's College of Agriculture and Life Sciences (CALS). Cornell CALS requires no AI statement and points faculty to Eaton's repository, a reflection worksheet and an "AI Course Policy Generator" (Cornell CALS FAQ).

ISU's gap is narrower than it first looked. The provost's template page has no AI wording, but CELT's "Gen AI Syllabus Considerations" document (found on the live re-check) supplies a three-approach vocabulary — "Permitted and encouraged use," "Limited use," "Prohibited use" — plus sample language, a list of required processes (verify accuracy, cite, reflect, "Disclose use of AI tools, prompts, generated outputs"), and alternatives for students who opt out (CELT). That puts ISU level with Berkeley and UF on vocabulary. The remaining gaps are a default for silence, assignment-level labels and a detector stance. CELT also runs a self-paced Canvas course on generative AI that "takes about two hours to complete" (September 12, 2024). In the article about it, Christine Denison's students "provide a disclosure note with every assignment to explain which AI they used, how they used it and affirm they are responsible for the result" (Inside Iowa State, 2024).

The national picture shows why support matters. Only 32% of institutions have a campus-wide AI policy, and only 23% of faculty consider such policies effective (Tyton Partners 2026). Globally, 80% of faculty report a lack of clarity about AI in teaching (Digital Education Council, Jan 2025).

The pattern is consistent. Peers have converged on a three-level course vocabulary, and the work that matters happens one level down. Berkeley's "Full AI" sample shows where grounded tools fit: "This is a GenAI-required course," with instructor-provided tools (Berkeley Senate). Ohio State's Committee on Academic Misconduct and Cornell both push assignment-level icons (OSU EHE; Cornell icons). Icons are the lightest way to fill a course-level template field without making it vague.

Silence is a policy, and peers that define it choose "no"

When a syllabus is silent, peers fall into four camps. Every institution that sets a crisp default sets it at "not allowed": - Maryland (approved January 15, 2025): "Students should assume that the use of GenAI tools to complete course assignments and assessments is not allowed unless otherwise specified in the course syllabus or assignment/assessment instructions" (UMD). - Rutgers (page modified September 1, 2026): AI tools "should not be considered permissible for coursework unless clearly stated or communicated by instructors" (Rutgers OIT).

Stanford is the outlier. Its 2023 Office of Community Standards guidance treats AI "analogously to assistance from another person" and bars using it "to substantially complete an assignment or exam" (Stanford OCS). Duke, CMU and Northwestern let their honor codes do the work: unauthorized assistance is misconduct, so silence leans restrictive. CMU's teaching center warns that unstated policies leave students unable "to effectively interpret what kinds of AI use are authorized" (CMU Eberly). No ISU default was found.

The behavioral evidence points the same way, with a caveat: it measures intentions, not behavior. Middlebury surveyed students from December 2024 to February 2025 (analysis n=634). 42.2% said they would likely use AI under no explicit policy, nearly the same as the 40.9% under "allowed if cited." Under a ban the figure fell to 13.4%, about 39 points below unrestricted use (Contractor & Reyes, arXiv).

EDUCAUSE's 2025 student survey found: - Students whose instructors prohibit AI are significantly more likely to say they do not use it. - Only 51% of students had received any AI guidance. - 52% said most or all of their instructors prohibit AI.

Source: EDUCAUSE 2025 via ACCJC.

A default alone is not enough. Middlebury students asked for specific examples of acceptable and unacceptable uses. Interview research found students using more than 20 rationalizations, and found their practice drifting from the instructor's intent at "at least five distinct sites" (Kim et al., arXiv 2026).

The design that answers both problems pairs a "no unless stated" default with an explicit allow-list. UW–Madison Biology 151 does exactly this, and its policy opens on an encouraging note: "We are excited about the use of AI within the fields of education and biology" (Bio 151 syllabus).

2025–26 syllabi split rules by assignment and shrink disclosure

The evidence comes from four sources: - Thirteen 2025–26 syllabi fetched directly from biology, animal science, statistics, economics, chemistry and intro CS courses. - Lance Eaton's crowd-sourced repository: 222 entries as of October 25, 2026. Writing courses dominate (39 entries). STEM, health and agriculture together are only about 15%, and only one entry is agriculture (Eaton sheet). - A peer-reviewed coding of 92 instructor policies: 52 allowed restricted use, 27 encouraged use with restrictions, 12 were zero-tolerance and 2 were unrestricted. About 90% placed responsibility for AI competency on the student (Tong et al. 2025 via NCA). - A SIGCSE 2025 study of 98 computing syllabi at 54 R1 universities. Most prohibited or restricted AI, and most placed the guidance inside the academic-integrity section (arXiv 2410.22281).

The dominant 2025–26 STEM pattern protects the proctored assessment rather than banning the tool: - MIT 14.05 "explicitly encourages" AI, requires students to cite it, and bans it on in-class exams (MIT OCW). - Wharton BEPP 6120 encourages experimentation, with a "closed book" and "closed computer" final as the backstop (Wharton). - UF ANS 3006 Online, the one animal-science example with an AI statement, says: "AI should not be used to assist with any quizzes. You can use AI for preliminary brainstorming on written assignments." It asks students to name the platform (UF ANS 3006). - UW CSE 373 lists allowed uses, for example "Generate code to complete tasks that you have already mastered from previous coursework," alongside banned ones (UW CSE 373). - Duke STA 199 separates code from prose. Students must "first attempt to solve the problem yourself" and may not paste AI-written narrative verbatim. Its principle: "Working with AI should not reduce your thinking ability" (STA 199). - Harvard CS50 permits only its own guarded tools: "Using CS50's own AI-based software, including the CS50 Duck (ddb)" is reasonable, while third-party AI is not (CS50 syllabus).

A restrictive minority persists, usually as department boilerplate. UF ECO 3203 bars AI on all graded work "unless their instructor has expressly granted that permission" (UF ECO 3203). UW Econ 200 is short and gives its reason: the assignments "are designed to assess your understanding and independent thinking" (UW Econ 200).

Silence and passing mentions persist too, sometimes inside one course. Georgia Tech CHEM 1211K's Fall 2026 lecture syllabus never mentions AI, while its lab syllabus says "Assignment submissions containing AI-generated text will be considered a violation of academic integrity" and that AI used to revise writing "should be disclosed and cited for transparency" (lecture; lab). An earlier section page that researchers quoted now returns "permission is required," so that quote was dropped. Silence is common even in strong agriculture departments. Only one of six publicly posted 2025 UF Animal Sciences syllabi has an AI statement, and the in-person section of ANS 3006, taught by the same instructor in the same term, has none (UF ANS 3006 in-person). No 2025–26 top-100 syllabus from food science, agronomy or agricultural economics with fetchable AI text turned up, so CALS-discipline examples remain a real gap.

Survey data put restrictive policies in the 2024 mainstream, with the trend moving away from them. In Ithaka S+R's February–March 2024 survey, 42% of US instructors completely prohibited student AI use (an earlier draft added "45% in the sciences"; the report's 45% figure is humanities faculty skepticism, so it was removed) (Ithaka S+R, June 2024). Tyton's June 2026 report found 47% of faculty have redesigned assessments, and 24% are "Integrators" who use oral exams, drafts and student critiques of AI output. Faculty rate AI-embracing policies effective twice as often as bans (Tyton 2026). That is a perception, not an outcome measure, but it is persuasive with skeptics.

ISU's own rules are the most permissive in this comparison, if read literally. ISU says that when logged in, "you can use it for confidential data" for Copilot, and Gemini and NotebookLM can be used "for confidential data too," while free ChatGPT is limited to data "classified as low" and "The university is working to add a license" (ai.iastate.edu). ISU's data page defines its four classes as Restricted, "High. Confidential student information protected by Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA)," Moderate and Low (ISU Data Services). Read together, "confidential" points at the FERPA class, which Harvard, Illinois and Penn State all exclude from the same tools. The AI page never says so explicitly, so confirm with ITS before any student work goes into a tool, and keep instructor-built tools to instructor materials.

Peers differ on that same tool. UIUC clears Gemini and NotebookLM only for "Internal and Public" data (Illinois). Penn State clears its Gemini Notebook only for Levels 1–2 (PSU).

Stanford's tool matrix shows how fast these approvals move (checked live October 25, 2026). Claude Code and Claude Cowork are approved when provisioned through "the UIT managed workspace, which was released on June 30, 2026," but the Claude for Education entry reads: "Although Anthropic has redeployed Claude Fable 5, this tool remains disabled in the Stanford enterprise environment as it does not currently align with the university's obligations for data governance, research protection, and institutional risk management" (Stanford UIT). The lesson for ISU: approval is per tool and per data class, and it can change mid-semester.

Across peers the risk boundary is clear: an instructor's own syllabus and lectures are rarely the problem; student work is. Harvard FAS (2023): "Faculty must get documented permission from students before putting original student content into any generative AI tool" (Harvard OUE; the page moved from its old address). The same page now announces that "Starting in Fall 2025," faculty can use the Respondus lockdown browser for in-person exams "to ensure that students do not use AI unless the course asks them to do so." CMU says FERPA protection begins once an instructor accepts an assignment for grading (CMU Eberly).

Peers have not settled the question of instructor materials. Purdue's teaching guidance says instructor-generated materials "should never be uploaded to any third-party site" (Purdue OVPTL), yet its tool list clears iSeek.ai for "course curricula and materials" (Purdue). Yale protects faculty IP from the student side, with a sample clause: "Do not submit instructor-created materials to AI tools without explicit permission" (Yale Poorvu). No US policy found says who owns or may reuse course materials once faculty upload them to an institutional AI tool.

The guardrail lessons are blunt. Malan's team set out to build a "less-helpful version of ChatGPT," found prompting insufficient, and added code that screens every response. Even so, the Duck emitted code blocks "up to a quarter of the time" before fixes, and some students sent about 200 questions each (Harvard Gazette, Sept 2026). Kestin's physics team found that "a system prompt could not reliably provide enough structure to scaffold problems with multiple parts," so the platform enforces it (Kestin et al. 2025). The cheapest guardrail an instructor controls is what goes into the index: UCSD lets instructors leave assignments and study guides out, and Maizey defaults to content students can already see.

The outcome evidence is thin but consistent: - Harvard physics tutor (crossover RCT, N=194): 0.63 SD over in-class active learning, with 0.73–1.3 SD by quantile regression. The tutor was heavily structured, built on expert solutions, and had authors who overlapped with the instructors (Kestin). - Chilean econometrics course (two pre-registered RCTs, about 300 students): a course-grounded GPT had no effect when merely offered (0.05 SD). Coaching students to use it tutor-style produced +0.22 SD and raised the pass rate 12.8 points. This is a preliminary working paper (Gallegos, IZA 2026). - Turkish high schools: unguarded GPT-4 left students 17% lower on unaided exams, while a hint-first tutor roughly neutralized the harm (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025). - Biology-textbook study (online RCT, N=124): the text-only grounded chat was rated most engaging but scored lowest on the post-test (Taneja et al. 2026).

So satisfaction surveys say nothing about learning. No RCT of an instructor-built Canvas-connector bot exists.

Conclusion

The peer frontier has moved past permission and is moving toward verification. Higher-ranked universities have largely settled what to say: a three-level vocabulary, a "no unless stated" fallback, short disclosure, and no detectors. None of them yet tells faculty how to check what an AI built from their own materials actually says. The systems with real evidence behind them, the CS50 Duck, Jill Watson and the Harvard physics tutor, earned it through checks and structure layered on top of prompts. The RCT literature says the same about students: access alone does nothing, and guided use helps. That makes the five-test audit more than a workshop exercise. It is the missing institutional practice, and ISU could standardize it before any peer does.

ISU's position is unusual. It is one of very few universities that require an AI statement in every undergraduate syllabus, and CELT already supplies a three-approach vocabulary with sample language. What is missing is a default for silence, assignment-level labels, a detector stance and a plain statement of which tools may hold FERPA data. That gap is an opening for CALS rather than an embarrassment. Pairing the Fall 2026 mandate with CELT's language, an honest data table and a verification practice would let ISU lead on the part of AI-in-teaching that peers have not reached. AI 2010 is the place to prove it with students.
