AI in course syllabi: what higher-ranked universities actually do

Seven findings, four quotable syllabus lines and a three-sentence AI statement any instructor can adapt, drawn from 2025–26 syllabi and policies at universities ranked above Iowa State.

Series CALS AI in Teaching, Iowa State UniversitySession "Your Course, Your Rules," Oct 26, 2026Checked against live sources Oct 25, 2026Scope universities ranked above ISU (#116, U.S. News 2027)
Read the full reportJump to the three sentences

Universities ranked above Iowa State have not agreed on one AI rule. They have agreed on a process. The institution supplies vocabulary and data rules, the instructor writes a course statement, and the real decisions happen assignment by assignment. In 2025–26 science and quantitative syllabi at MIT, Wharton, UW, UF and UW–Madison, the common pattern is: AI allowed for practice and learning, banned on proctored quizzes and exams, use disclosed in a line or two, and the student owns every error. Wherever an institution has set a default for a syllabus that says nothing, that default is "not allowed" (Maryland, Rutgers, Michigan State). The best student evidence shows that silence otherwise reads as permission. AI detectors have been switched off or disavowed almost everywhere.

The frontier is grounded course tutors. The two best-documented ones, Harvard's CS50 Duck and Georgia Tech's Jill Watson, both found that prompts alone cannot hold the guardrail, which is exactly what an instructor-run audit of a course tutor checks. No peer agriculture college publishes its own AI-in-teaching policy. No peer publishes an instructor workflow for "answer only from my materials." Instructors who ground a tutor in their own materials and audit it are ahead of the peers, not catching up.

Seven findings for instructors

# Finding Peer evidence What to do
1 Silence reads as permission Middlebury students' stated likelihood of using AI: 42.2% under no policy, 40.9% under "allowed if cited," 13.4% under a ban (self-reported intent, surveyed Dec 2024–Feb 2025) (Contractor & Reyes) A syllabus with no AI policy still tells students something
2 Where a default exists, it is "no" Maryland (Jan 2025): students "should assume" AI is "not allowed" unless told otherwise (UMD); Rutgers and MSU say the same (Rutgers; MSU). No published ISU default was found Add one default line to every syllabus
3 Split the rules by assignment, not by tool MIT 14.05, Wharton BEPP 6120, UF ANS 3006 and UW CSE 373 all allow AI for practice and ban it on proctored work (MIT; UW) Restrictive syllabi can keep exams AI-free and still open study uses
4 Short disclosure beats transcripts Georgia Tech BIOL 1207L (Fall 2026) asks four questions: tool, how, why, how verified (GT). Only 31.3% of Middlebury students know how to cite AI (arXiv) Permissive syllabi can trade "full responses" for a link plus a verification step
5 Detectors are out, and student work is the data risk MSU turned off Turnitin's AI detection (MSU). Penn says uploading student work to a detector without consent violates FERPA (Penn CETLI) Build only from your own materials; no student work
6 The prompt is not the guardrail The CS50 Duck produced code "up to a quarter of the time" despite instructions not to (Harvard Gazette). Jill Watson answers only when verified course material exists: 78.7% accuracy vs 30.7% for a generic assistant (GT) An instructor's five-test audit is the classroom version of their code checks
7 Structure and coaching produce learning; access alone does not Harvard physics tutor: +0.63 SD vs active learning (Kestin 2025). Access alone had no effect, coaching gave +0.22 SD (Gallegos 2026). Unguarded GPT cut exam scores 17% (Bastani 2025) Every artifact ships with a three-line "how to use this" note for students

Three syllabus sentences, assembled from peer language

May be used for. You may use ISU-licensed AI tools (logged-in Copilot, Gemini or NotebookLM) or this course's study guide to review concepts, quiz yourself and get feedback on drafts, after you have first attempted the work yourself.

May not be used for, and why. You may not use AI on quizzes and exams, or to write the interpretation you submit in lab reports, because those assignments are designed to assess your own understanding and independent thinking.

Not authoritative. AI output is not an authoritative source. It can fabricate credible-looking data, references, quotations and calculations, so check anything you use against course materials. You are responsible for every error you submit.

Where each sentence comes from: - Sentence 1 draws on UW CSE 373's allow-list, Missouri BIOSC 3750's list of roles (editor, translator, idea generator, data visualization tool, tutor) and Duke STA 199's "first attempt to solve the problem yourself." - Sentence 2 draws on MIT 14.05, UF ANS 3006, and UW Econ 200's rationale (UW Econ 200). - Sentence 3 draws on Missouri microbiology and Ethan Mollick, both undated entries in Eaton's repository (Missouri's rank relative to ISU was not verified), plus UChicago Harris.

Optional fourth line: "If an assignment does not say, assume AI is not allowed. Where it is allowed, add one line: tool, what for, and how you checked it."

Four common starting points, and one upgrade for each

If your syllabus has… Peer example One upgrade
No AI policy 5 of 6 publicly posted 2025 UF Animal Sciences syllabi had no AI statement (UF ANS 3043). ISU's required Fall 2026 template already expects an instructor AI-use policy for undergraduate courses (Inside Iowa State) Write the three sentences plus the default line
A restrictive policy UF ECO 3203 department boilerplate (UF); Texas A&M ISTM 705 (undated, Eaton) Add the reason, plus a four-part permission request (assignment, tool, intended use, reason) and the study uses that stay open
A permissive policy requiring tool, exact prompts and full responses Boston University Computing & Data Sciences: appendix with "the entire exchange" (undated, Eaton) Switch to GT BIOL 1207L's four questions or a shared chat link (UW–Madison LIS 408); students keep logs and produce them on request (UMN)
AI mentioned only in passing Georgia Tech CHEM 1211K (Fall 2026): the lecture syllabus never mentions AI (lecture), while the lab syllabus for the same course has a full policy: AI "can be a useful tool for learning and/or improving your writing, however, it should not serve as a substitute for your own understanding" (lab) Grow the passing mention into the lab version: what AI is for, what counts as a violation, and "should be disclosed and cited for transparency"

What Iowa State already has. ISU does publish AI syllabus guidance, with three approaches, not four. CELT's "Gen AI Syllabus Considerations," linked from its AI in Teaching page, offers "Permitted and encouraged use," "Limited use" and "Prohibited use." It adds six considerations (approach, tools, conditions, required processes, rationale, alternatives), sample language for each approach, and a step most peers skip: alternatives for students who choose not to use AI (CELT document). The required Fall 2026 ISU template asks for "Instructor policies for attendance, late work, extra credit and use of AI" (Inside Iowa State). What ISU still lacks: a default when a syllabus is silent, assignment-level labels, and a written stance on AI detectors. Start from the CELT document; the three sentences above fit inside its "Limited use" approach.
