Part 4 · Judging RAG · Chapter 17

Why judging RAG is hard

In one sentence: a written answer has no answer key, so judging it means asking several separate questions, and each of them can be answered by a person, by a list of facts, or by another language model.

Why you should care

In Part 2 a search result was easy to score. A document was relevant or it was not, and a formula did the rest. A written answer is different. Two good answers to the same question can share no sentence. A wrong answer can read better than a right one. If you cannot judge answers, you cannot tell whether a change to your system helped, and every later choice is a guess. This part of the book is about how the field judges RAG today, and how far each method can be trusted.

Words you need

Assessor
A person who judges system output for an evaluation. At TREC, assessors work for NIST.
Rubric
A written list of what a good answer must contain or do, made before the answers are read.
Coverage
How many of the facts that matter the answer contains.
Support
Whether the cited document really says what the sentence claims.
Pairwise judging
Showing a judge two answers to the same question and asking which is better.
Bradley–Terry rating
A way to turn many wins and losses between pairs into one rating per system, much like a chess rating.
Fluency
How well the text reads. It says nothing about whether the text is true.

Core The idea

Four reasons it is hard

  • There is no answer key. You cannot compare an answer to one correct text, because many different texts are correct.
  • Fluent is not the same as right. Modern models write well almost always. Readers, and judges, are drawn to good writing. A smooth answer with one invented fact can beat a clumsy correct one.
  • An answer can be true and still unsupported. RAG promises that each sentence rests on a document. A true sentence with a citation that does not back it breaks that promise.
  • Judging is slow and people disagree. A careful person needs minutes per answer, and two careful people often give different verdicts.

Split the one big question into three

“Is this a good answer?” is too vague to judge the same way twice. The field splits it.

QuestionHow it is judgedChapter
Does the answer contain the facts that matter?A list of facts, called nuggets, is written first. Then each answer is checked against the list.18
Is each sentence backed by its citation?Each sentence is read next to the cited document.19
Would a reader prefer this answer to that one?Two answers are shown side by side and a judge picks one.20

The first two give a score per answer. The third gives only a winner per pair, so it needs one more step, a rating method such as Bradley–Terry, to become a ranking of systems.

Who does the judging?

Each of the three questions can go to a person or to a language model. People are the reference, and they are scarce. A track with a hundred runs and a hundred topics has ten thousand answers. No budget pays for people to read them all. So evaluations mix the two: people do the part that needs judgment most, and models do the bulk work. Chapters 20 and 21 ask how far the model part can be trusted.

One score is not enough

Chapter 4 said that one number is never enough for search. It holds here even more. An answer can cover every fact and cite badly. It can be well supported and miss the point. A report on your system should give coverage, support and preference separately, and say who or what did the judging.

How we did it at TREC 2026

We did not judge the RAG track. We were judged by it. The organizers scored the generation runs in four ways, and looking at the same two runs through all four shows why one number is not enough.

  • Side by side, by people. NIST assessors compared answers in pairs. The wins and losses became Bradley–Terry ratings. Only each team's two highest-priority runs were compared this way.
  • Rubrics written by assessors from the topic and their own knowledge (batch 1, 17 narratives).
  • Nuggets drawn by assessors from the documents (batch 2, 25 narratives).
  • Nuggets made automatically by the AutoNuggetizer method, for the same 25 narratives.

In the last three, a language model decided which items each answer fully supports. So people wrote two of the three lists, and a model did all the checking.

All four views put both runs above the middle of the field, and all four put the full-text run a little ahead. They do not agree on how far ahead. Under people's side-by-side choices the two runs are close, and the uncertainty ranges of their ratings overlap. Under automatic nuggets the gap is large. Chapter 22 tests which of these gaps is more than luck.

What surprised us

The same answers scored 0.66 and 0.37. On the same 25 narratives, our full-text run fully supported 66% of the vital nuggets that assessors wrote and 37% of the vital nuggets that the automatic method wrote. The answers did not change. The list of facts did. For the whole field the middle score falls from 0.49 to 0.15. So a nugget score is a statement about an answer and a list together. It has no meaning as an absolute level, and scores from two different lists must never be put side by side.

A second point: only 42 of the 119 narratives we answered were scored at all, 17 in one batch and 25 in the other. Human judging is the scarce resource, and every evaluation is built around that fact.

Try it

  1. Pick a question you know well. Get answers from two different systems or two different prompts.
  2. Give each answer one mark from 1 to 5 for “overall quality”. Put the answers away for a day and mark them again. Did your marks hold?
  3. Now write the five facts a good answer must contain, before looking at the answers again. Count the facts in each answer.
  4. For each sentence with a citation, open the source and check that it says so.
  5. Compare the three results. Which one would you trust to guide a change to the system?

Check yourself

  1. Why can a written answer not be scored against one correct text?
  2. Name the three separate questions that replace “is this a good answer?”.
  3. What does a Bradley–Terry rating do?
  4. In the 2026 RAG track, which part of the judging did people do, and which part did a language model do?
  5. Our run scored 0.66 with one nugget list and 0.37 with another. What does that tell you about nugget scores?
Answers
  1. Many different texts are correct answers to the same question.
  2. Does it contain the facts that matter? Is each sentence backed by its citation? Would a reader prefer it to another answer?
  3. It turns many pairwise wins and losses into one rating per system.
  4. People compared answers side by side and wrote the rubrics and one set of nuggets. A model wrote the other nugget set and decided which items every answer supports.
  5. The score depends on the list as much as on the answer, so only scores from the same list can be compared.

Go deeper

  • Older measures. ROUGE and BLEU count the words an answer shares with a reference text. They are cheap, and they punish a correct answer that uses different words. They are rarely used for RAG now.
  • Ceilings. When most good systems score near the top of a measure, as with the assessor rubrics here, the measure can no longer tell them apart. Look at the spread of the field before trusting a gap.
  • Ratings are relative. A Bradley–Terry rating depends on which systems were in the pool. Add or remove systems and every rating moves.
  • What the user wanted. None of these methods asks whether the answer helped a real person finish a real task. That needs user studies, which are slower still.
  • Project idea. Take 20 questions and two systems. Judge them by nuggets, by citation checks and side by side. Report where the three methods agree and where they do not.
