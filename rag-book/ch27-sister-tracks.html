Part 5 · TREC and the 2026 tracks · Chapter 27

The sister tracks: RAGTIME and Auto-Judge

In one sentence: RAGTIME asks for cited reports from news in four languages, Auto-Judge asks for programs that score RAG answers, and together with the RAG track they form one loop: two tracks produce answers and the third tests how well machines can judge them.

Why you should care

The three tracks share ideas, file formats and people. If you learn one, the other two cost much less to enter. They also show the same problem from three sides. The RAG track asks how to answer a question from a large English collection. RAGTIME asks the same when the evidence is in languages the reader cannot read. Auto-Judge asks who can be trusted to mark the answers.

Words you need

RAGTIME
The RAG TREC Instrument for Multilingual Evaluation. A track on writing reports from documents in several languages.
Report request
RAGTIME's kind of topic. It has a title, a background that says who is asking, a problem statement, and a length limit.
MLIR
Multilingual information retrieval: one ranked list that mixes documents from all the languages.
Request decomposition
Splitting a report request into the questions a good report should answer. RAGTIME's new side task in 2026.
Host track
A track whose system outputs are handed to Auto-Judge to be judged.
Leaderboard
The list of systems with one score each, from best to worst.
Anonymized run
A run with the team and system names removed, so a judge cannot know who wrote it.

Core The idea

RAGTIME

RAGTIME began in 2025. It grew out of an earlier TREC track on search across languages, called NeuCLIR. It is organized by people at Johns Hopkins University, the University of Glasgow and Microsoft.

The collection is news from the web, collected between August 2021 and July 2024. It has four languages: Chinese, English, Russian and, new in 2026, Spanish. Each language has about one million documents. The report is always written in English. So a good system must find a Russian article, understand it, and state its content in English with a citation that points back to the Russian original.

The 2026 track had three tasks.

  • Report generation, the main task. For each of 103 report requests, write a report split into sentences, each with its citations.
  • Multilingual retrieval. Return up to 1,000 documents per request, from all four languages in one list.
  • Auto-nuggetization, new in 2026. Write the list of questions a good report should answer, before any report exists (Chapter 18).

Auto-Judge

Auto-Judge does not ask for answers. It gives you other teams' answers and asks you to score them. The official statement of the task is short: given all system runs for a track, predict an evaluation score for each system and produce a leaderboard. It is organized by people at the University of New Hampshire, Johns Hopkins University and RMIT University, with advisers and with contact persons from each host track.

A pilot round used anonymized runs from TREC 2025. The 2026 test data, released on 26 August 2026, came from two host tracks:

A team submits two things: the leaderboards its judge produced, and the judge program itself, so that the organizers can run it again. A judge is then scored by how well its leaderboard matches the official one (Chapter 21). For the pilot, the official leaderboard of the host track was the reference. For the test, the reference is human assessment.

How the three fit together

  1. 1RAG trackTeams answer 119 narratives from an English web collection.answers
  2. 2RAGTIMETeams write 103 reports from news in four languages.reports
  3. 3Auto-JudgeTeams build judges that score the anonymized answers and reports of both.scores
  4. 4PeopleHuman assessors judge a sample. Their results test the systems and the judges.the reference

The links are written down by the tracks themselves. The RAG and RAGTIME output formats are standardized, so one judge can read both. The three websites point to each other. Some organizers work on two of the tracks.

The small rows matter most. A system built for the RAG track and pointed at RAGTIME without changes would count its length in the wrong unit and would put its best citation in any position. Both mistakes cost points without any warning.

How we did it at TREC 2026

We entered all three, and work moved between them.

  • From RAG to RAGTIME. The writing steps were shared: evidence in numbered blocks, one sentence per claim, a validator before upload (Chapter 12, Chapter 16). What had to change was the unit of length, the citation order, and retrieval across languages (Chapter 11).
  • From RAGTIME to judging. Writing nugget questions for the RAGTIME side task taught us how much a nugget list depends on its writer (Chapter 18). That lesson shaped how we built judges.
  • Our own answers were in the pile. The Auto-Judge test data holds the anonymized runs of the RAG and RAGTIME tracks. Our runs for those tracks are among them, and so our judges scored our own answers without knowing which ones they were.

What surprised us

The practice score in Auto-Judge is random on purpose. The test package comes with a reference leaderboard so that your code can run from start to finish. That reference is a random placeholder. The track's instructions say that the correlations you see locally are meaningless by design. It is a simple and strong protection: no team can tune a judge on the test data, even by accident, because there is nothing real to tune against. The same instructions tell teams that the judge may read everything and the people may not. It also means you learn how good your judge is only when the organizers tell you.

RAGTIME had its own surprises, all in the guidelines for anyone who reads to the end. Nuggets are called opinions, like relevance judgments. Documents are assumed to be truthful. A correct, well-cited sentence that matches no nugget is ignored: it earns nothing and costs nothing.

Try it

  1. Open the RAGTIME site and the RAG track site. Find, for each, the length limit and its unit. How would you check both in one validator?
  2. Take one answer you wrote for an earlier lab. Change it to fit RAGTIME's rules: one sentence per item, citations in order of strength, 5,000 characters. What did you have to cut?
  3. Read the task statement on the Auto-Judge site. List what a judge receives and what it must return.
  4. Discuss: your own system's answers are among the runs your judge will score. What could go wrong, and how does anonymizing help?

Check yourself

  1. In which language is a RAGTIME report written, and in which languages are the documents?
  2. Name the three RAGTIME tasks of 2026.
  3. What does an Auto-Judge team submit?
  4. Name two rules that differ between the RAG track and RAGTIME and that a validator should check.
  5. Why is the local reference leaderboard in the Auto-Judge test package random?
Answers
  1. The report is in English. The documents are in Chinese, English, Russian and Spanish.
  2. Report generation, multilingual retrieval, and auto-nuggetization (request decomposition).
  3. The leaderboards its judge produced for every run in the data set, and the judge program itself.
  4. The length limit (1,024 words against 5,000 characters) and the citation rule (up to three against a ranked list where the first one counts most).
  5. So that nobody can tune a judge against the true answers. The real comparison is made by the organizers.

Go deeper

  • Automatic scoring inside RAGTIME. The 2025 track scored reports with a tool called Auto-ARGUE, run with a Llama 3.3 model. The Auto-Judge resource paper points out what follows: a judge built on the same model family may agree with that reference for the wrong reason. This is the circularity of Chapter 21.
  • Why a separate track for judges? The Auto-Judge proposal notes that each track had adopted its own preferred judge. A shared track lets the same judges be compared across tracks.
  • More than rank agreement. The Auto-Judge materials list three kinds of comparison: agreement of leaderboards, agreement of single labels, and agreement of preferences between pairs of answers.
  • Statements of absence. In RAGTIME a sentence that says the collection does not report something can earn credit, if a nugget states that absence.
  • Other RAG tracks. The Auto-Judge pilot also used runs from the 2025 DRAGUN and BioGen tracks. Both are worth a look if your interest is news trust or medicine.
  • Project idea. Take ten answers from an earlier lab and score them with a judge you write. Then shuffle the system names and score again. Does anything change?
