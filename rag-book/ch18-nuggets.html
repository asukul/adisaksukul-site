Part 4 · Judging RAG · Chapter 18

Nuggets

In one sentence: a nugget is one fact a good answer should contain, and scoring by nuggets means writing the list of facts first and then counting how many each answer holds.

Why you should care

Nuggets are the main tool for judging the content of RAG answers at TREC. They solve the problem of Chapter 17: two answers with no sentence in common can both be checked against the same list of facts. Once the list exists, any number of answers can be scored with it, today or next year. If you build one evaluation tool for your own project, build this one.

Words you need

Nugget
One fact, or one question with its answer, that a good response to the topic should include.
Vital nugget
A fact the answer must have to be good.
Okay nugget
A fact that is useful and not essential.
Nuggetization
Writing the list of nuggets for a topic.
Nugget assignment
Deciding, for one answer, which nuggets it contains.
Strict score
A score that counts a nugget only when the answer supports it fully. Partial support earns nothing.
Nugget recall
The share of the nuggets that the answer contains.
AutoNuggetizer
A method from the TREC RAG organizers in which a language model writes the nuggets and assigns them.

Core The idea

Two steps

  1. 1Write the listFor each topic, list the facts a good answer should contain. Mark each one vital or okay.once per topic
  2. 2AssignFor each answer, go down the list. Does the answer support this nugget fully, partly, or not at all?once per answer
  3. 3ScoreCount the supported nuggets and divide by the size of the list.a formula

An example

Topic: why did the city close the old bridge? The list has four vital nuggets and two okay ones.

NuggetKindIn the answer?
An inspection found cracks in the steelvitalfully
The bridge closed in Marchvitalfully
Repair would cost more than a new bridgevitalfully
Buses now take the north roadvitalpartly: says only that buses were moved
The bridge was built in 1931okayfully
A footbridge stays openokayno
vital strict = fully supported vital nuggets ÷ all vital nuggets = 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75
all strict = fully supported nuggets ÷ all nuggets = 4 ÷ 6 = 0.67
These are the two measures the 2026 RAG track reported. In the second one, vital and okay nuggets count the same.

Other versions exist. A weighted score gives an okay nugget half the weight of a vital one, or gives half credit for partial support. Always say which version you report.

Where does the list come from?

  • From an assessor's own knowledge of the topic. Quick, and limited to what that person thinks of.
  • From the documents. An assessor reads relevant documents and pulls out the facts. Slower, and tied to what the collection holds.
  • From a language model. The model reads the topic and some documents and writes the list. Cheap enough for every topic.

The 2026 RAG track used all three, on different sets of narratives. That lets us compare them below.

What nuggets do not see

A nugget score measures recall of facts. It does not notice a false statement, a missing citation or a badly ordered answer. An answer that states every nugget and also ten wrong things gets full marks. This is why nuggets are always paired with a support check (Chapter 19).

Two things stand out. The six runs come out in exactly the same order under both lists. And every number in the second column is far lower than its partner in the first. The two lists agree on which system is better and disagree on how good any system is. With only six runs, the matching order is a description and not a proof. The papers above test it on a whole track.

Writing nuggets ourselves

RAGTIME had a side task: write the nugget questions for each of its 103 topics, before any report exists. We submitted five lists of 25 questions per topic. One came from a GPT model that saw the top eight search snippets. One came from Claude models run as coding agents. Two came from Gemini 2.5 Flash, one of them with snippets and one with the request alone. The fifth merged the first two: it took questions from the two lists in turn and skipped any question whose set of words overlapped by three quarters or more with a question already kept.

What surprised us

Two strong models, the same topics, almost no shared questions. We compared the wording of the questions from the GPT list and the Claude list. Of the 2,575 GPT questions, only 111, or 4.3%, had a close match in wording in the Claude list for the same topic. Different words can ask the same thing, so the true overlap in meaning is higher than that. Even so, the merge had to skip only 48 near-copies across all 103 topics. Two capable writers, given the same request, chose different facts to ask about. A nugget list is one writer's view of what matters, whether the writer is a person or a model. The RAGTIME guidelines say the same about human nuggets: they are opinions, like relevance judgments.

Try it

  1. Choose a topic. Without looking at any answer, write eight nuggets and mark each vital or okay.
  2. Ask a classmate, or a language model, to write eight nuggets for the same topic. How many match yours?
  3. Score two answers against your list, then against the other list. Compute vital strict and all strict.
  4. Do the two lists agree on which answer is better? Do they agree on the scores?

Check yourself

  1. What are the two steps of nugget evaluation, and which is done once per topic?
  2. An answer fully supports 5 of 8 vital nuggets and 1 of 4 okay nuggets. Compute vital strict and all strict.
  3. Name one thing a nugget score cannot see.
  4. Our six runs had the same order under assessor nuggets and automatic nuggets, with very different scores. What may you conclude, and what not?
  5. Why did our two nugget lists from two models overlap so little?
Answers
  1. Writing the list, once per topic, and assigning the nuggets to each answer.
  2. Vital strict is 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625. All strict is 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5.
  3. False statements, missing or wrong citations, or poor organization.
  4. The two lists rank these systems alike. You may not compare a score from one list with a score from the other, and six runs are too few to prove the lists always agree.
  5. Each writer chose different facts as the ones that matter. A nugget list is one view of the topic.

Go deeper

  • The pyramid method. Several people write nuggets, and a nugget named by more people gets more weight. It reduces the effect of one writer's taste and costs several times the work.
  • Automatic lists are harder. In our scores every system did much worse on the automatic list. A likely reason is that a model writes many narrow nuggets from the documents it was shown, so an answer built from other documents misses them. The score files do not tell us the cause.
  • System-level and topic-level agreement. Automatic nuggets can rank systems well on average and still be wrong on single topics. Use them to compare systems, not to grade one answer.
  • Scoring a nugget list. The RAGTIME side task scores a list by how many of the assessors' nuggets its questions match, with measures such as subtopic recall.
  • Nuggets that need citations. RAGTIME counts a nugget only when the sentence that states it is also correctly cited. That joins this chapter to the next.
  • Project idea. Write nugget lists with three different models for 20 topics. Measure how much the ranking of five systems changes with the list.
