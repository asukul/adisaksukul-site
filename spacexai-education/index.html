The plan

Five parts, one front door

SpaceXAI has the products and the capital to become the platform students learn on. What it lacks is the relationship with the people who decide what gets taught. This program builds that relationship and keeps it.

Access

The educator is the front door

Verify the teacher once. The teacher applies for each class, and students redeem a capped coupon. Fraud drops because a named professional is accountable.

Tracks

Cursor builds, Grok does everything else

Cursor serves computing, engineering and data. Grok and Grok Bot serve business, agriculture, health, education, media, the sciences and the humanities.

Material

Written by the faculty who teach it

Experts from each discipline write workflow templates, assignments and exams that let students use AI and still do real work by hand.

Community

Recognition educators value

Certification, fellowships, speaking invitations and a conference, funded through a nonprofit so that public employees can take part.

Public

Build with Grok

A public library where people share workflows built with Grok and Grok Bot. Anyone can reuse them, and creators earn when they do.

Why educators

Students follow the tools their courses use

Educators choose those tools, and most are choosing without training, policy or material. A platform that solves their problem becomes the default in their classroom, term after term.

SpaceXAI starts from strength. Grok Bot reached about 418,000 weekly users five weeks after its August launch (PYMNTS), and Cursor is a tool professional developers already pay for. What is missing is the path from those products into a syllabus. An education program is how the tools reach the next group of users before their habits are set, and it is cheap next to what the company already spends on everything else.

Instructor's view

Every platform gets one half right

An instructor needs two things at once: a tool every student can use without paying, and material that is ready to teach from. Among the platforms I teach with, only one gives me both, and it is a cloud platform, not an AI assistant.

Only Google Cloud pairs open access with material ready to teach

Positions are my judgment as an instructor who teaches with these platforms. They are not measurements.

What I will hold this program to

Three rules that close the gap the others leave open.

  1. Access and material are designed together. No lesson needs more than the classroom credit provides. If a student cannot run it without paying, it does not ship.
  2. A curriculum, not a collection. Ordered units, each with slides, a lab, an assignment and an assessment, for every level from school courses to professional training.
  3. A request an instructor can finish between classes. About ten minutes to apply, and credit in students' hands within days.

The result for SpaceXAI: students meet Grok, Grok Bot and Cursor first, in a course, with a teacher, before any other AI platform. First choices tend to hold. In one 2025 survey, 91% of AI users said they go to their favourite tool first for nearly every job (Menlo Ventures).

Strict about

  • Who the educator is, checked once and by a person where needed
  • One coupon per student per course
  • A cap on each coupon, and expiry at the end of term
  • No transfer or resale of credit
  • Usage visible to the educator and to the program

Flexible about

  • Which subjects qualify: any accredited course, not only computing
  • Who counts as an educator: adjuncts, community college instructors, and teaching assistants with a faculty sponsor
  • Speed: small classes approved automatically, large ones within days
  • Top-ups when a class runs out mid-term
  • School email domains outside .edu, for K-12 and international partners

What I have seen as an instructor. I have taught with Google Cloud, AWS, Azure and other platforms for more than fifteen years, and I have watched fraud hit free student offers on several of them.

Fraud is a design problem. It follows offers that anyone with a student email can claim alone. It is far less likely when an educator, with a reputation and a career to protect, asks on behalf of a class.

Strictness has a cost too. Where access is very strict, I could rarely get credit into a legitimate course. Google Cloud's lighter process let my students use credit every term, and they went on to learn the wider family of tools around it: Gemini, AI Studio, NotebookLM, Flow and Stitch. I want to find the same balance for Grok and Cursor.

Grok Bot in the classroom

The instructor publishes the bot. Students learn by supervising it

The controlled setup, step by step

This is how I would run a section of 30 to 100 students today, on a departmental Teams or Enterprise tenancy.

Department

One tenancy per course

Students join a course team with institution accounts. Admin sets template sharing to team-only and turns on audit logs and action recording.

Instructor

Build the bot on practice systems

The bot connects to a sandbox: a sample CRM, a mailbox of synthetic tickets, a demo storefront. No real student or university data.

Instructor

Write the rules

Auto Review rules in plain language: ask before sending anything, never spend money, never leave the allowed sites. On Enterprise, enforce them team-wide.

Instructor

Publish as a Team Bot

Every student gets the same bot with private memory. Changes made during the term reach everyone at once.

Students

Run, audit, fix, report

Students run the task, read the action recording, find what went wrong, tighten the rules, and hand in a supervision report.

Layers of control, and which plan has them

Every platform that puts agents in classrooms uses the same three layers: an admin enables the feature for a group, the maker restricts what the agent may use, and the runtime stops for approval on sensitive actions. Grok Bot has the third layer everywhere, the maker layer on every plan, and the admin layer almost entirely on Enterprise: audit logs, action recording, enforced Auto Review, MCP allowlists and network allowlists are Enterprise features in the current docs (docs).

How the other platforms do it

The pattern a Grok Bot classroom will be measured against.

  • Microsoft: an instructor shares an agent with a "Can chat" role to a group, admins must approve wider publishing, and under-13s are blocked (Microsoft Learn).
  • OpenAI: workspace agents for Edu let admins choose which groups may build and share agents and which tools agents may touch, with approval on sensitive actions (release notes).
  • Google: a teacher creates a Gem from Classroom and posts it to the class, and can see how students used it (Google).
  • Instructure: the Canvas IgniteAI Agent previews each step before acting, and AI is switched on by institution, department or course (Constellation).

None of them publishes independent evidence of learning outcomes from an action-taking agent in a course. That is open ground for this program.

Grok Bot for students: a four-rung ladder

Students should not start by building agents. They should start by catching one making a mistake. The ladder runs from using a bot the instructor made to publishing one of their own on Build with Grok.

1
Use

Run the instructor's Team Bot on a practice task and write up what it did and did not do.

2
Supervise

Read the action recording, find the error, tighten the Auto Review rules, and rerun.

3
Configure

Adapt a template for a new process: new instructions, new routine, new connected tool.

4
Build and publish

Design a team of bots for a client task and publish it as a template, with a supervision guide.

Two cautions carry into the design. Reliance on AI tracks a student's trust and satisfaction more than their competence, in a controlled experiment where the assistant was sometimes deliberately wrong (Pitts and colleagues, 2025), and a review of 70 studies found less revision and lower post-test scores where AI did the work (Delikoura and colleagues, 2025). The ladder is built to counter both: the student's grade comes from what the bot got wrong and what they did about it.

Build with Grok

Classrooms reach students. Build with Grok reaches everyone else. It is a public library where people share the workflows and templates they built with Grok and Grok Bot, and where anyone can reuse one for their own work in a single click. Sharing is free, and proven creators can charge a small fee. It builds on the template sharing and the creator rewards pilot that Grok Bot already has. I can lead it alongside the educator network, with one community across both.

Why this fits where Grok is heading

On October 7, 2026, Elon Musk posted that Grok Bot will send each task to whichever model does it best, including models from other companies.

"Going forward, @SpaceX will use the best back end model for any given task."

As reported by The Next Web, which names Claude Opus 5.5, Midjourney and Suno. The post covers Grok Bot and gives no start date. The October 11 changelog describes models falling back to Grok by region, which suggests routing is rolling out, but the other companies had not confirmed it in the reporting I read (changelog).

The workflow becomes the product

If the best model is chosen automatically, people stop choosing a model and start choosing a workflow. Grok does not have to win every benchmark. It has to be the best place to build, and a shared library of workflows is what makes it that.

Templates outlast models

A template says what the work is, not which model does it. When a better model arrives, every shared template improves without its creator or a teacher rewriting anything.

One classroom credit, every model

The instructor's problem above was that the best material sat behind another company's paid plan. If classroom credit runs through Grok Bot, a student could reach the best model for each task with one coupon.

Two things to settle first. Calls to outside models cost SpaceXAI money, so classroom credit and creator rewards need caps that allow for it. And schools must know which company receives student work, so a class needs a way to see and limit the providers a template may use. Reporting says SpaceXAI has not yet explained how users will control this (TechRepublic).

Built in from the first day

  • Points count only real reuse by distinct verified accounts, with limits on new accounts
  • An originality check before points or fees pay out. Copies earn nothing
  • Templates never carry the creator's accounts, credentials or data
  • Published review criteria, and a reason given for every rejection
  • Votes choose the contest shortlist and a panel chooses the winners, so a vote ring cannot buy a prize
  • Rewards paid mostly in Grok usage, which keeps people building and costs less than its face value
  • Creators keep ownership of what they share, and choose its licence
  • Contest rules, paid listings and tax handling reviewed by counsel. Adults only until an education mode exists

How it connects to classrooms

  • Every scenario in the library above becomes a template, credited to the faculty fellow who wrote it
  • Educators publish lesson templates that another instructor can load into a class in one click
  • A classroom bracket in the monthly contest, where a verified course enters together
  • Faculty fellows and certified educators on the review and judging panels
  • Students leave a course with published templates as a portfolio
  • One community forum for creators, students and educators

Nonprofit home

Run fellowships and travel through a nonprofit

Many educators are state or federal employees. Ethics rules limit what they may accept from a company that sells to their institution. A company-sponsored, tax-exempt organization gives them a way to take part. This is a year-two question, not a condition for starting; the pilot needs none of it.

This is not legal advice. Nonprofit status helps but does not settle the question by itself. Under Iowa's gift law, for example, what matters is whether the giver contracts or seeks to contract with the employee's agency, though expenses tied to speaking or serving on a panel are a recognized exception (University of Iowa summary). The foundation should make grants and never sell. SpaceXAI should sell campus licences separately, and counsel should review the rules state by state.
